Nodes, lesions, colonies, reads. Poisson, its overdispersed cousin, and the road to RNA-seq.

Day 2 · Topic 5 · Practical statistics for biomedical researchers
Counts are everywhere in the lab: cells per field, colonies per plate, lesions per animal, reads per gene. They're whole numbers, never negative, and their spread grows with their size. One new idea today: overdispersion, extra variation beyond pure counting noise. It's the reason RNA-seq tools use the negative binomial.

The lecture
Start with α at 0: a Poisson distribution, the counts you'd get if every subject had exactly the same true rate and only random sampling varied. Its variance equals its mean. Move μ: the spread grows with the average. Then move α up: the negative binomial. Same mean, but more zeros, a longer tail, and variance = μ + αμ². That α is overdispersion. Next slide shows where it comes from.

Where overdispersion comes from.

VARIANCE / MEAN OF THE COUNTS
The lecture · the biology behind α
Step 1: 300 mice, all with exactly the same true rate (5 lesions on average). Their counts still differ, by chance alone: that's Poisson noise, variance/mean ≈ 1. Step 2: realistic mice differ in their true rate (genetics, age, cage). The top strip shows their rates spreading out. Step 3: the counts now spread much more than Poisson predicts: variance/mean well above 1. The negative binomial is exactly this: Poisson counting on top of subject-to-subject variation in the rate.

Count models

exp(b) is a rate ratio: the expected count is multiplied by it. "1.7 times as many nodes" is what it means.

Logistic (this afternoon)

exp(b) is an odds ratio. "Times as likely" is only right for rare outcomes.

Poisson and negative binomial give almost the same rate ratios. They differ in how uncertain they say those ratios are.

The lecture
Same structure as logistic regression: a straight line on a log scale, exponentiated coefficients as ratios. With overdispersed data, Poisson assumes less noise than there is: CIs too narrow, p-values too small, false positives.

Debrief · section B · concept check
Purple: Poisson; pink: negative binomial. The point estimates almost coincide; the intervals don't. The Pearson statistic should be about 1 if Poisson fits; here it's around 5 for GBSG2, meaning the true variance is about five times what Poisson assumes, so Poisson's standard errors are about √5 ≈ 2.2 times too small. That's the concept check. On simulated data, the pink truth ticks show the NB intervals catching the built-in rate ratios.

Debrief · section C
Bars: observed counts. Lines: the distribution each fitted model predicts (averaged over all subjects). Poisson is too narrow: too few large counts. NB matches the spread and the tail. On GBSG2, both predict some zeros although there are none: a reminder that a model only knows what you tell it; how the data were collected (only node-positive patients) is outside it. A zero-truncated model would be the principled fix.

Debrief · section D · discussion question 2
This is the bridge to the last hands-on. Both axes are logarithmic. The dashed line is variance = mean (Poisson). At low expression, genes follow it: with a handful of reads, sampling noise is all you see. At high expression, they climb above it, following variance = mean + α·mean²: replicate cell lines genuinely differ. That's why DESeq2 and edgeR use the negative binomial, and why they estimate α per gene while borrowing strength across genes: with 4 replicates, one gene alone can't pin down its own dispersion.

Four things to keep.

  1. Counts: model the log of the expected count; exp(b) is a rate ratio.
  2. Biological counts are overdispersed: variance much larger than the mean.
  3. Poisson on overdispersed data gives CIs that are too narrow: use the negative binomial.
  4. Check what a model predicts against the data, and how the data were collected.
