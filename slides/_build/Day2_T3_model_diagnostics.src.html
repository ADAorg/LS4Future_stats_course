Model diagnostics.

A model always gives you numbers. Four pictures tell you whether to believe them.

Day 2 · Topic 3 · Practical statistics for biomedical researchers
The model from this morning will fit any data, including data it describes badly. Diagnostics are how you check, and the main tool is looking at what the model got wrong: the residuals.

What the model assumes, most important first.

critical

1 · Independent observations

One row per independent unit. Repeated measures on the same mouse, or cells from one dish, break this. No plot catches it: it's about the design.

critical

2 · The right shape

A straight line (or whatever form you wrote) describes the trend. If the truth curves, every estimate is off.

important

3 · Same spread everywhere

The noise is as large for big fitted values as for small ones. If not, the standard errors, CIs and p-values are wrong.

least, with large n

4 · Roughly normal noise

Matters for small samples. With hundreds of observations, the central limit theorem (Day 1) covers most departures.

And one practical check: no single point is in charge. If removing one observation changes your conclusion, you need to know.

The lecture
Note the order. People worry most about normality because it has a famous test, but it's the least important with decent n. Independence is the most important and no residual plot can show it; it's a design question (Day 3 touches on it, and the paired designs on Day 1 were an example). Shape and spread are what the plots are for.

Residuals: what the model missed.

The lecture · the key idea
Step through. A residual is the vertical distance from a point to the fitted line: observed minus predicted. Step 3 tips the plot over: x becomes the fitted value, y becomes the residual, and the fitted line becomes the horizontal line at zero. That is the residuals-vs-fitted plot, the single most useful diagnostic. A healthy one looks like a shapeless band around zero.

Four plots, four questions.

This is what healthy looks like: simulated data with a straight-line truth and well-behaved noise.

  • Residuals vs fitted: is the shape right? Look for a curve.
  • Normal Q-Q: is the noise roughly normal? Look for points leaving the line.
  • Scale-location: is the spread constant? Look for a rising trend.
  • Cook's distance: is one point in charge? Look for a spike.

The red smooth lines help your eye; don't over-read small wiggles.

Debrief · section B concept check
Concept check: a coefficient of 0.10 on log(outcome) means the outcome is multiplied by exp(0.10) ≈ 1.105, about 10% higher, per unit of the predictor. Drag the slider to show where the "100 × b" shortcut breaks down (the curve leaves the dashed line beyond about ±0.2). This is the same multiplicative reading you'll use for odds ratios and rate ratios this afternoon.

Three things to keep.

  1. Look at residuals, not just p-values: four plots, every model.
  2. Shape and spread matter more than normality; plots beat tests.
  3. Fix, don't discard: transform, robust SEs, check flagged rows, report what you did.
