Time to event.

Censoring, Kaplan–Meier curves, the log-rank test, and what a hazard ratio does (and doesn't) mean.

Day 3 · Topic 1 · Practical statistics for biomedical researchers
On Day 1 (choosing a test, card 5) we hit a wall: a t-test on survival times with 56% of patients still alive. Today we climb it. Three ideas: censoring is partial information, not missing data; Kaplan–Meier uses it properly; the hazard ratio is a rate ratio, and the most misread number in clinical papers.

Censored is not missing.

The lecture · censoring
Fourteen simulated patients. Step 1: real studies recruit over months or years, so patients start on different calendar dates. Step 2: the analysis happens on one date; anyone still alive is censored, as is anyone lost to follow-up. Step 3: survival analysis re-aligns everyone at their own start: time since entry. Now the censored patients sit at all follow-up lengths, not only at the end. Step 4: the faded dashes are what we would see if we could wait forever. A censored time is a lower bound: "survived at least this long". Throwing it away, or treating it as a death, both distort the answer.

The lecture · notebook section A
200 simulated patients, true median survival 24 months (dashed). Recruitment runs through the first half of the study. Shortcut 1 treats every censored time as a death: far too pessimistic. Shortcut 2 drops censored patients: even worse, because the ones dropped are exactly the long survivors. Kaplan–Meier tracks the truth at every study length; at 12 and 24 months it says "median not reached", which is the honest answer. Ask the room: at 120 months, why do the shortcuts nearly work? (Almost nobody is censored.)

Kaplan–Meier, one step at a time.

The lecture · notebook section B
Ten patients, four event times. At each event time ask one question: of those still at risk right now, what fraction got through? Multiply those fractions. A censored patient counts in the denominator up to the moment they leave, then simply drops out: no step in the curve. That's all Kaplan–Meier is. The median is where the curve first reaches 50%.

GBSG2: hormone therapy vs none, recurrence-free survival. Lung: women vs men, overall survival. Always report survival at a fixed, clinically meaningful time with the median; when the median isn't reached, the fixed-time number is all you have.

The lecture · notebook section C
At each event time, the patients at risk are a mix of both groups. If the groups had the same hazard, events should land in each group in proportion to its share of the risk set: that's the expected count. Press play and watch the gap open: in GBSG2, hormone-therapy patients have fewer events than expected all along. The log-rank statistic is (O − E)² / V, compared with a χ² distribution. The t-test p-value on the observed times is smaller here, but it is meaningless: observed time mixes survival with how long each patient happened to be followed.

A hazard ratio is a rate ratio.

Hazard ratio Events by 5 years, control

HR 0.7: at any moment, among patients still event-free, the event rate is 70% of the control rate. Not "30% fewer events", not "live 30% longer".

The lecture · notebook section D
The hazard is the event rate among those still at risk, per unit time. Proportional hazards means the treated curve is the control curve raised to the power HR. Fix HR at 0.7 and move the control event rate. When events are rare by 5 years, the risk ratio is close to 0.7. When most control patients have the event, the risk ratio shrinks towards 1 (everyone eventually has the event in both groups), and the absolute difference first grows then shrinks. Same story as odds ratios yesterday: a relative number needs a baseline to become something a patient can use.

Debrief · section D
Cox regression is to survival what linear and logistic regression were yesterday: exp(coefficient) is a hazard ratio, and each one is adjusted for the others in the model. Start with Simulated: crude HR 0.93, nothing to see; add age and it drops to 0.64, near the true 0.6. The drug went to older patients, and age raises the hazard, so the benefit was hidden. GBSG2 is the contrast: the therapy groups differ in menopausal status (Day 1) but adjusting barely moves the HR, because a confounder must be tied to both the treatment and the outcome. The lecture · notebook section E (optional)
Proportional hazards: the HR is the same at every time point (right panel flat). Early harm, late benefit: surgery, aggressive chemotherapy. The curves cross; a log-rank test and a single Cox HR average the harm and the benefit and can find nothing. Effect wears off: the curves separate and then run parallel. In both cases report the curves, or HRs for separate time windows, rather than one HR. In GBSG2, tumour grade III fails the Schoenfeld test (p = 0.003): its effect is strongest early.

The good answer comes from the Kaplan–Meier curves on slide 5, computed here from the data. "Live 30% longer" confuses a ratio of rates with a ratio of times; under very specific assumptions (constant hazards) an HR of 0.7 corresponds to median survival about 1.4 times longer, which is not 30% either.

Four things to keep.

  1. A censored time is information: "at least this long". Never drop it or call it an event.
  2. Kaplan–Meier + numbers at risk; report survival at a fixed time as well as the median.
  3. Hazard ratio = ratio of event rates among those still at risk, assumed constant over time.
  4. Translate HRs into absolute survival for patients; adjust in Cox like any regression.
