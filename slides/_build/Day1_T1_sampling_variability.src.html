Sampling variability.

Your study is one draw from many possible studies. How much could the answer have wobbled?

Opening (1 min). Today is about one idea that everything else this week builds on: your data are one sample, and a different sample would have given a different answer. Confidence intervals, p-values and power are all ways of putting a number on that wobble. Every chart in this deck is a live simulation on the real GBSG2 data, the same data as in the notebooks.
The problem

Every number you report is an estimate.

You want a fact about a population (all patients, all cells of that type). You only ever measure a sample.

The most common mix-up in papers

Two spreads, two questions.

Standard deviation · SD

How different are patients from each other?
A property of the biology. More patients won't make it smaller.

Standard error · SE

How much would my mean move if I repeated the study?
A property of the design: the spread of the sampling distribution. It shrinks as n grows. How fast? You'll find out.

Error bars: ± SD to show variability, ± SE (or a CI) to show precision. Always say which.

Same 20 patients, two error bars. Mean ± SD says "patients vary by about ±14 mm". Mean ± SE says "if I repeated the study, my mean would typically move by about ±3 mm", the spread of the histogram on the previous slide. Ask: which one should go on a figure? Depends on the message, but always say which. A bar chart with ±SE bars can make noisy data look very precise. In practice we don't have 2,000 studies; we estimate the SE from one sample. Drag the slider. The histogram is 2,000 freshly simulated studies at each n; the pink bracket is ±1 SE. The practical punchline: 10 → 40 mice halves your SE; to halve it again you need 160. Diminishing returns are built into statistics, which is why sample-size planning (Hands-on 5) matters. In real life σ is unknown, so we plug in the sample SD: SE ≈ s/√n.
Concept check 1 · the central limit theorem

Averages forget the shape of the data.

Start with positive nodes at n = 1: the "means" are just patients, so the histogram is as skewed as the data. Step up n: by 30 the means sit almost exactly on the bell curve (pink line), even though the data never do. That's why tomorrow's CIs and t-tests work for non-normal data with decent n. The caveat for small studies: at n = 5 with skewed data the bell curve is still off, which is why confidence intervals in Hands-on 2 cover a bit less than 95%.

The SE and every CI or p-value built on it only describe random noise; they say nothing about how patients got into your study. AI as tutor · check its answer

Does the tutor know SD from SE?

Prompt from the notebook:

I measured tumour size in 20 patients. The sample mean is 31 mm and the sample standard deviation is 14 mm. What is the difference between the standard deviation and the standard error here, and which one tells me how precise my estimate of the mean is?

Score it on four points:

correctcompletespecificactionable

A good answer says:

  • SD describes patients; SE describes the estimate
  • SE = 14 / √20 ≈ 3.1 mm
  • more patients shrink the SE, not the SD

Watch for:

  • "report the SE because it's smaller"
  • SE as the variability of patients
  • no number, only definitions (not specific)
Ask two or three pairs to read out how they scored their AI answer. Most current models get the definitions right; the misses are usually in "specific" (no actual SE calculated) and "actionable" (no advice about which to report on a figure). If someone's answer recommended SE "because it looks cleaner", use it: that's a reporting choice, not a statistical reason.
Take home

Three things to keep.

  1. Your result is one draw. A repeat study would give a different number.
  2. SD describes patients. SE describes your estimate, and shrinks with √n.
  3. More data shrinks noise, never bias.
