Adjustment and interactions.

What "adjusted for age" really means, and how to ask whether an effect is different in different people.

The lecture · what adjustment does
This is the simulated dataset from the notebook, rebuilt in the page: 300 people, a biomarker that rises with age, and genotype BB adds exactly 8 units. The twist: BB carriers in this sample happen to be about 8 years older. Step 1: compare the two averages, you get about 13, too big. Step 2: BB carriers are older. Step 3: older people have higher values, so part of the 13 is age. Step 4: fit two parallel lines (one model: biomarker ~ genotype + age) and measure the gap between them at the same age. That gap is the adjusted effect, close to 8. Take your time on step 4: "adjusted for age" means "compared at the same age".

Adjusting = comparing like with like.

Another way to see it: split people into age bands and compare BB with AA within each band.

Inside every band, the difference is close to the truth. Pooling everyone together is what inflates it.

The regression does this in one step, without cutting age into bands: it compares groups at every age at once.

The lecture · the same idea, without equations
Each purple dot is the BB − AA difference within one age band, with its 95% CI. They scatter around 8 (the truth), not around the crude 13. This is how epidemiologists adjusted before regression was easy: stratify, compare within strata, then combine. The regression's adjusted estimate is essentially a weighted average of those within-band comparisons. Bands are coarse; the model uses age exactly.

The lecture · the recipe for a confounder
Flip the two switches. Each combination rebuilds the world (2,000 people) and fits both models. Only when age is linked to the group AND to the outcome do crude and adjusted disagree. If BB carriers aren't older, adjusting changes nothing (but costs nothing either). If age doesn't matter for the biomarker, being older changes nothing. Both arrows: confounding. This is the concept-check sentence in the notebook.

So adjust for everything? No.

Confounder: adjust

Age is linked to who is in the group and to the outcome, but it isn't caused by the group. Adjusting removes a false part of the difference.

Mediator: don't adjust (for the total effect)

The drug lowers blood pressure, which prevents strokes. Adjusting for blood pressure compares patients with the same pressure, hiding the very route by which the drug works.

The data can't tell these apart: both are "related to group and outcome". Only knowledge of the biology, and of what happened first, can.

The lecture · discussion question 1
This is the limit of statistics alone. A confounder sits before both the group and the outcome. A mediator sits between them, on the causal path. Adjusting for a mediator answers a different question ("does the drug work through some route other than blood pressure?"), which can be interesting but isn't the total effect. Ask the room for one confounder and one mediator from their own work.

Interaction: does the slope depend on the group?

biomarker = b₀ + b₁·age + b₂·male

Additive: one slope for everyone; men are shifted up or down by b₂. Parallel lines.

biomarker = b₀ + b₁·age + b₂·male + b₃·age·male

Interaction: women's slope is b₁, men's slope is b₁ + b₃. Fanning lines.

Debrief · section B · the idea
A cartoon of the two models before we look at data. The extra term age·male is zero for women, so their slope is b₁; for men it adds b₃ per year, so their slope is b₁ + b₃. The interaction coefficient is the difference in slopes. Clinical translation: "does the drug work better in men?" is an interaction question: drug × sex.

Four things to keep.

  1. "Adjusted for age" = groups compared at the same age.
  2. Adjusting changes an estimate only if the variable is linked to both group and outcome.
  3. Adjust for confounders, not mediators: that's biology, not statistics.
  4. An interaction is a difference in slopes, and needs a lot of data to pin down.
