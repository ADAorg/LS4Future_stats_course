The lecture · notebook section A
The pink dots are the observed share of "yes" in bins of tumour size: that's what we want to model. A straight line ("linear probability model") rushes through the pink zone above 1: a 129% chance makes no sense. The logistic model bends to stay between 0 and 1, and follows the observed proportions, flattening at the extremes. How it does that is the next two slides.

One chance, three scales.

Probability of "yes"

Only the log-odds scale runs from −∞ to +∞ with no walls. That's where the model can draw a straight line.

The lecture · step 1 of 3
Drag the slider. 20 patients, the pink ones have the outcome. Probability = pink out of all 20. Odds = pink per grey: "5 to 15" is 1 to 3, odds 0.33. Odds go from 0 to infinity; at 50% they're exactly 1. Log-odds stretch that to the whole number line, symmetric around 0 (50%). Bookmakers and epidemiologists think in odds; the model thinks in log-odds; patients and clinicians think in probabilities.

A straight line on log-odds is an S on probability.

The lecture · step 2 of 3
Step 1: the model is an ordinary straight line, but on the log-odds scale. Step 2: watch the same line as the y-axis is converted back to probabilities: it bends into the S-curve, flattening near 0 and 1 because those walls can't be crossed. Step 3: the observed proportions sit on the curve. Nothing new mathematically: same b₀ + b₁x as this morning, read through a different lens.

exp(b) is an odds ratio.

Tumour size

Every 10 mm multiplies the odds by the same factor, wherever you start. The change in probability depends on where you start.

The lecture · step 3 of 3
Drag the slider. The two dots are a patient at size x and one 10 mm larger. Their odds ratio stays fixed (exp(10 × b₁)) along the whole curve. But the gap in probability is small at the flat ends of the S and largest in the steep middle. That's why an odds ratio alone doesn't tell a clinician "how much more likely": you need a starting probability.

Odds ratios are not risk ratios.

Odds ratio Risk in reference

In the workshop datasets (crude, two groups):

OR ≈ RR only when the outcome is rare (under ~10%) in both groups. For common outcomes the OR is further from 1, so "times as likely" overstates the effect.

Debrief · section D · concept check
Fix the odds ratio at 2.5 and slide the reference risk. When the outcome is rare (a few percent), the risk ratio is almost 2.5 too. As it becomes common, the risk ratio falls towards 1, because a probability can't exceed 100% while odds can grow without limit. The icon arrays show it: at 32% baseline (GBSG2 grade I), OR 2.5 means about 54% vs 32%, a risk ratio of 1.7, not 2.5. The lung row is the most dramatic example: an OR of about 2.7 corresponds to a risk ratio of 1.3.

Four things to keep.

  1. Yes/no outcomes: model a straight line on the log-odds scale.
  2. exp(coefficient) = odds ratio, constant across the predictor.
  3. Probability gaps are not constant: report predicted probabilities too.
  4. OR ≈ RR only for rare outcomes. Otherwise, don't say "times as likely".
