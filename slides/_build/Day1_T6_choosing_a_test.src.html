Card 1 · Two independent groups.

Do pre- and postmenopausal GBSG2 patients differ in tumour size? 290 vs 396 different patients.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 1
The easy card, on purpose. Different patients, so no pairing. Tumour size is skewed, but with about 300 per group the central limit theorem from this morning has done its job: Welch and Mann–Whitney agree that there's no clear difference. They ask slightly different questions (means vs a shift in the whole distribution).

Card 2 · The same mice, twice.

Tumour volume (mm³) in 10 mice before and after two weeks of treatment. The AI prompt calls them "groups".

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 2 · built-in AI failure point
Click Welch first: the lines disappear, because an unpaired test throws away which "after" belongs to which "before". The big mouse-to-mouse differences swamp the treatment effect. Then paired: every single mouse went down, and the test only looks at those within-mouse changes. Same numbers, conclusions orders of magnitude apart. Ask: did your AI notice they were the same mice? What word in the prompt misled it?

Card 3 · Proportions, small numbers.

Tumours developed in 1 of 10 mice on a protective diet and 6 of 10 control mice.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 3
The chi-square p-value relies on an approximation that needs expected counts of about 5 or more in every cell. Here the expected tumour counts are 3.5 per group, so we use Fisher's exact test, which counts every possible arrangement of the 7 tumours among the 20 mice. Note that the "right" test lands above 0.05 while the approximate one lands below. That's not a reason to prefer either; the design decides. Report the proportions (10% vs 60%) whichever test you use.

Card 4 · Skewed data, one outlier.

Serum IL-6 (pg/mL) in 8 healthy controls and 8 patients. Cytokines are typically right-skewed; one patient is very high.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 4
Watch the y-axis change with the test. Welch works on the raw values: the 82.9 outlier inflates the patients' SD and the test loses almost all its power. Mann–Whitney replaces values by ranks: 7 of 8 patients rank above every control. The log scale (common for concentrations) tames the skew and the outlier. Both of those are defensible if chosen in advance; the problem is choosing after seeing which p-value is smallest. On the Gemini prompt: with 8 values a normality test has almost no power; a plot tells you more.

Card 5 · The trap: unfinished stories.

Do patients with hormonal therapy stay recurrence-free longer? time is days to recurrence or to the last follow-up visit.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 5 · built-in AI failure point · preview of Day 3
More than half the patients were still recurrence-free when follow-up ended (open circles): we only know their time is longer than recorded. A t-test or Mann–Whitney treats every open circle as if the recurrence happened that day. Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test use each patient only while we actually know their status. Here the p-values happen to agree on direction, but they answer different questions, and with heavier censoring they can disagree badly. Discussion question 3: even with the right test, Hands-on 3 showed these groups differ in age and menopausal status, so the log-rank difference is not a clean treatment effect. Day 3.

Same data, different answers.

In cards 2, 3 and 4 the choice of test moves p across 0.05, in both directions.

Choosing the test after seeing the p-values is p-hacking, even when each test is "valid" on its own.

Write the analysis plan first: outcome, test, and what you will do with skewed or censored data.

AI as tutor · discussion question 1
The lesson generalises: the AI answers the question as described. It can't see your design unless you describe it, and the details that matter (pairing, censoring, how a variable was measured) are exactly the ones people leave out of a prompt. Ask the room for their best rewritten prompts.

Day 1 in six lines.

  1. Your result is one draw; the SE says how much it could wobble.
  2. A 95% CI comes from a method that catches the truth 95% of the time.
  3. p = how surprising the data would be if there were no difference. Not more.
  4. Test enough things and something will be "significant".
  5. Small studies miss real effects and exaggerate the ones they find.
  6. The design picks the test, and you must describe the design to the AI.
