Parametric or non-parametric?

PARAMETRIC · e.g. t-test

  • Assumes a distribution family (usually normal) described by a few parameters: mean, SD.
  • Tests a statement about a parameter: "the means differ".
  • Uses the actual values, so one extreme value can dominate.
  • Most powerful when its assumptions hold; with large n the central limit theorem makes it robust.

NON-PARAMETRIC · e.g. Mann–Whitney

  • No distribution family assumed: works on the ranks (the order) of the values.
  • Tests whether values in one group tend to be larger than in the other.
  • An outlier is just "the largest value": robust to skew and outliers.
  • Not assumption-free: observations must still be independent. Reading it as "medians differ" needs similar shapes.

The lecture · parametric vs non-parametric
"Parametric" means the test assumes the data come from a family of distributions pinned down by a few parameters, and it makes its inference about those parameters. The t-test assumes normality within groups and compares means. Rank-based tests throw away the values and keep only their order. That buys robustness and costs a little information. Two common misunderstandings to flag now: non-parametric is not "no assumptions" (independence still matters, which is why card 2 needs a paired test of either kind), and Mann–Whitney is not in general a test of medians. Tomorrow (Day 2) you'll see that Mann–Whitney is almost exactly a t-test on the ranks.

Mann–Whitney, step by step.

The lecture · how a rank test works
Tumour volume at day 14 in 7 control and 7 treated mice (illustrative numbers). One treated mouse has a very large tumour. Step 2: the t-test summarises each group by mean and SD; the outlier drags the treated mean up and inflates its SD, so p = 0.49. Step 3: replace every value by its rank among all 14. Step 4: Mann–Whitney's U simply counts, over all 7 × 7 = 49 control–treated pairs, how often the control mouse had the larger tumour: 41 of 49. U / 49 estimates the probability that a random control mouse beats a random treated mouse. Step 5: if treatment did nothing, every arrangement of the 14 ranks is equally likely; that gives the exact null distribution of U, and 41 or more extreme happens with p = 0.038. Step 6: make the outlier even bigger: the t-test gets worse, Mann–Whitney doesn't move, because the ranks didn't change. Step 7: without the outlier, both tests agree.

The lecture · simulation, 1,000 experiments per point
Each point: 1,000 simulated experiments with the given shift between groups; power = share with p < 0.05. At effect 0 the curves show the false-positive rate. Normal data: the t-test wins, but only just: Mann–Whitney has about 95% of its efficiency, a small price for insurance. Skewed (log-normal) data with a multiplicative effect: Mann–Whitney clearly wins (a t-test on the log scale would do even better: that's card 4). Outliers (10% of values much more variable): the t-test's SD is inflated and its power collapses; Mann–Whitney barely notices. Now set n = 3: Mann–Whitney can never reach p < 0.05, because with 3 vs 3 the smallest possible two-sided p is 0.1. Rank tests need a minimum sample size.

Concept check · before the cards
Statement 4 is the strategy many people use, and it has problems: the normality test is underpowered exactly when n is small (when it matters) and flags trivial departures when n is large (when it doesn't), and choosing the test after peeking at the data distorts the error rate. Decide from what you know about the measurement: skewed by nature (concentrations, counts, times)? outliers likely? how small is n?

Debrief · card 2 · built-in AI failure point
Click Welch first: the lines disappear, because an unpaired test throws away which "after" belongs to which "before". The big mouse-to-mouse differences swamp the treatment effect. Card 3 · Proportions, small numbers.

Tumours developed in 1 of 10 mice on a protective diet and 6 of 10 control mice.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 3
The chi-square p-value relies on an approximation that needs expected counts of about 5 or more in every cell. Here the expected tumour counts are 3.5 per group, so we use Fisher's exact test, which counts every possible arrangement of the 7 tumours among the 20 mice. Note that the "right" test lands above 0.05 while the approximate one lands below. That's not a reason to prefer either; the design decides. Report the proportions (10% vs 60%) whichever test you use.

Card 4 · Skewed data, one outlier.

Serum IL-6 (pg/mL) in 8 healthy controls and 8 patients. Cytokines are typically right-skewed; one patient is very high.

p-VALUE–
Choose a test.
Debrief · card 4
Watch the y-axis change with the test. Welch works on the raw values: the 82.9 outlier inflates the patients' SD and the test loses almost all its power. Mann–Whitney replaces values by ranks: 7 of 8 patients rank above every control. The log scale (common for concentrations) tames the skew and the outlier. Both of those are defensible if chosen in advance; the problem is choosing after seeing which p-value is smallest. On the Gemini prompt: with 8 values a normality test has almost no power; a plot tells you more.

Card 5 · The trap: unfinished stories.

Do patients with hormonal therapy stay recurrence-free longer? time is days to recurrence or to the last follow-up visit.

Debrief · card 5 · built-in AI failure point · preview of Day 3
More than half the patients were still recurrence-free when follow-up ended (open circles): we only know their time is longer than recorded. A t-test or Mann–Whitney treats every open circle as if the recurrence happened that day. Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test use each patient only while we actually know their status. Here the p-values happen to agree on direction, but they answer different questions, and with heavier censoring they can disagree badly. Discussion question 3: even with the right test, Hands-on 3 showed these groups differ in age and menopausal status, so the log-rank difference is not a clean treatment effect. Day 3.

Choosing the test after seeing the p-values is p-hacking, even when each test is "valid" on its own.

Write the analysis plan first: outcome, test, and what you will do with skewed or censored data.

Day 1 in six lines.

  1. Your result is one draw; the SE says how much it could wobble.
  2. A 95% CI comes from a method that catches the truth 95% of the time.
  3. p = how surprising the data would be if there were no difference. Not more.
  4. Test enough things and something will be "significant".
  5. Small studies miss real effects and exaggerate the ones they find.
  6. The design picks the test, and you must describe the design to the AI.
