The planning problem
The two curves are the tumour sizes we'd expect without and with the drug: the treated curve is the GBSG2 distribution shifted 5 mm to the left. They overlap almost completely. If you picked one treated and one untreated patient, the untreated one would have the larger tumour only a bit more often than half the time. Cohen's d puts the effect in units of patient-to-patient spread; around 0.35 here, which is typical for clinical effects.

The theory behind the curve
Grey curve: what trial estimates look like if the drug does nothing. Purple curve: what they look like if it truly shrinks tumours by 5 mm. The dashed lines are where an estimate becomes "significant", fixed by α in the grey world. Power is the purple area beyond the line. Drag n: both curves get narrower (SE shrinks with √n, Topic 1), they separate, and the purple area beyond the line grows. A larger effect separates them faster.

Notebook sections C and D · discussion question 2
Each point is 2,000 simulated trials on the real, skewed tumour sizes; the dashed line is the textbook formula, which assumes normal data. They're close, not identical. Step the effect to 3 mm: the curve flattens and the required n explodes; at 10 mm a small trial is enough. α = 0.01 (the Gemini prompt) shifts every curve down: fewer false positives, less power. Discussion: where does the expected effect come from? Pilot data (noisy), literature (often inflated, see next slide), or better, the smallest effect that would matter clinically.

Notebook section E · discussion question 3
All 5,000 trials estimate a true 5 mm effect. With 15 per group, only the trials that happened to overestimate it cross the significance line (pink): if only significant results get published, the literature reports an effect much bigger than the truth. With 150 per group nearly every trial is significant, and the average significant estimate is close to the truth. This is why a surprisingly large effect from a small study should make you more cautious, and why effect sizes taken from the literature for power calculations are often too optimistic.

Three things to keep.

  1. Power = how often a real effect is detected. Plan for 80% before you start.
  2. Required n grows with 1 / effect²: the expected effect is the key assumption.
  3. Underpowered studies miss real effects, and exaggerate the ones they find.
