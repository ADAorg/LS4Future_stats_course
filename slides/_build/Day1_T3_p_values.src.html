What the number means
Drag the slider: it's a hypothetical observed difference between two groups. The p-value is simply the share of chance differences (pink tails, both sides) at least as extreme. Point out that the t-test, which uses a formula instead of shuffling, gives almost the same number. At 2.2–2.3 mm we cross p = 0.05: chance alone would produce a gap this big about 1 time in 20. Nothing magic happens at that line.

The p-value answers the other question.

What p is
P( data this extreme | no difference )

"If the groups were the same, how often would chance give a gap this big?"

What people read
P( no difference | data )

"How likely is it that the groups are the same?" p cannot tell you this.

Same trap, from the clinic: P(positive test | healthy) is not P(healthy | positive test).

A p-value also does not tell you:

  • how big the difference is, or whether it matters
  • that p > 0.05 means "no difference" (absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence)
The classic misreading
The p-value is calculated assuming no difference, so it can't also be the probability of that assumption. The diagnostic-test analogy usually lands: a test with a 5% false-positive rate does not mean a positive patient has a 95% chance of being ill; that depends on how common the disease is. We'll come back to this on the false-positives topic.

In a properly randomized trial

Baseline differences are chance by design, so a baseline p-value only tests the coin. CONSORT advises a baseline table without p-values, and judging imbalance by size, not significance.

Debrief · notebook section B · discussion question 3
At 50 per group almost no trial is significant, and the intervals are wide enough to include both "no effect" and "a big effect": inconclusive, not negative. At 50,000 per group every trial is significant with a tiny p-value, but the interval sits entirely inside the "too small to matter" zone. With enough patients, any non-zero effect becomes significant. The effect estimate and its CI tell you whether it matters. The 3 mm threshold is only an example; in practice it comes from clinicians.

Watch for:

  • "3% probability the null is true" or "97% chance the effect is real"
  • "the difference is large" or "clinically important"
  • no mention of the effect size and its CI

A good answer says:

  • if there were no difference, a gap this large would be unusual (about 3 in 100)
  • report how big the difference is, with its CI
AI as tutor · check its answer
Collect one good and one flawed answer from the room. A common pattern: the definition is right in the first paragraph, and the "practical implications" paragraph then reads p as the probability the finding is real. For the calculator check: a chi-square on age or a t-test on menopausal status would be a silent error. The code runs; the answer is wrong.

Three things to keep.

  1. p = how often chance alone gives a result this extreme, assuming no difference.
  2. It is not the probability that there is no difference, and not a measure of size.
  3. Always report the effect and its CI; check groups are comparable before comparing outcomes.
