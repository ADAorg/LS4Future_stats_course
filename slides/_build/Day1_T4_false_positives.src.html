Your coin-flip experiments all live in the left column: there was no effect, so every "significant" result was a false positive. α isn't a property of the data; it's the false-positive rate you agree to accept, decided before you look. Notebook section B · concept check
Run with no effect: the histogram fills evenly, every bar at about the dashed line. So the share below any threshold equals the threshold: that's why α is the false-positive rate. Switch α to 0.01 and 0.10 to see it hold. Zoom to 0–0.1 (the Gemini prompt): still flat. Then switch the true effect to 3 or 6 mm and run again: now p-values pile up near zero. Notebook section C
Each row is a study, each square a biomarker with no real effect. Pink squares are false positives. With 1 test, about 1 study in 20 has a hit. With 20 tests, most studies have at least one. With 100, almost all. Then switch to Bonferroni (α divided by the number of tests): the share of studies with any hit drops back to about 5%, at a price in power. An RNA-seq experiment runs about 20,000 tests: that's Day 2's false discovery rate.

Discussion question 2 · where this bites in your own work
Nobody runs 20 identical tests on purpose. But many analyses drift: a different readout, only the postmenopausal patients, only high-grade tumours. Each looks reasonable on its own. Click "New coin flip" a few times; then "How often" to see what share of pure-noise trials offer at least one publishable-looking cell. The tests overlap, so it's not exactly 1 − 0.95²⁸, but it's still high. Remedies: decide the primary outcome and analysis in advance, report everything you tried, treat subgroup findings as hypotheses.

One of 20 biomarkers, p = 0.04: what does the tutor say?

🎓 Tutor prompt from the notebook:

I measured 20 serum biomarkers in treated and control mice. One of them, IL-6, differed with p = 0.04. Can I conclude that the treatment affects IL-6?

Score it on four points:

correctcompletespecificactionable

🧮 Calculator check: after zooming the histogram to p between 0 and 0.1, are the bars still roughly flat?

A good answer mentions:

  • 20 tests → about 1 false positive expected
  • a correction (Bonferroni: 0.05 / 20 = 0.0025) or FDR
  • replication in an independent experiment
  • the size of the IL-6 difference, not only p

Watch for:

  • "yes, p < 0.05 so the effect is significant"
  • a correction suggested, but not applied to the numbers
AI as tutor · check its answer
Most current models raise multiple testing here, because the prompt says "20 biomarkers". A useful follow-up for pairs who finish early: remove "20 serum biomarkers" from the prompt and just say "IL-6 differed with p = 0.04". Does the tutor ask how many other things were measured? Usually not, and neither do reviewers.

Three things to keep.

  1. With no effect, p-values are flat: α is the false-positive rate you accept.
  2. Many tests, or many ways of analysing, make some hit almost certain.
  3. Decide the main analysis in advance; correct, replicate, and report everything you tried.
