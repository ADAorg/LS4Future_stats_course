INTERVALS THAT CAUGHT THE TRUTH–
From sampling variability to intervals
This is the one idea of the session. Top: the sampling distribution from the last topic, with the middle 95% in purple. Click "Run a study": a study's mean drops out of that distribution and we draw the same width either side of it. Purple mean → the interval reaches the dashed truth line. Pink tail → it misses. So "95% of means land near the truth" becomes "95% of intervals reach the truth". For now the width is fixed because we're pretending we know σ; next slide does it properly.

The recipe: mean ± t × SE.

In a real study you can never do this check. Here we can, because we know the population.

Why t and not 2? With few patients the SD is itself noisy, so the multiplier grows to pay for it.

Notebook section A
Walk through the numbers once: sample mean, sample SD, SE = s/√n, the t multiplier, the interval. Point at the t table: at n = 5 the multiplier is almost 2.8, by n = 30 it's close to 2, which is why "± 2 SE" is a decent mental shortcut for moderate samples. Notebook section C · discussion question 2
Fix n = 30 and step the level from 80% to 99.9%: more intervals catch the truth, but every one of them gets wider, and near 100% the width explodes. Then fix 95% and step n: width falls with √n, as in the last topic. The only free lunch is more data. Discussion: a pilot can live with 80–90% (you want a rough idea), a pivotal trial is judged at 95% or stricter. Show the 50 intervals at 99.9% with n = 5 for comic effect.

When the 95% promise breaks.

The t-interval assumes study means are roughly normal. With small samples of skewed data, they aren't yet (Topic 1, central limit theorem).

In your own small experiments:

  • more replicates if you can
  • log-transform skewed measurements (fold changes, concentrations)
  • bootstrap or non-parametric intervals
  • say so: "n = 4, interval approximate"
Notebook section D · discussion question 3
Click the button: each point is 10,000 simulated studies of nominal 95% t-intervals. Age is nearly symmetric and sits close to 95% even at small n. Tumour size is skewed and falls short at small n. Positive nodes is very skewed and falls short most. This explains the pooled number on the room slide. The fix list on the right is practical: most bench experiments are small, and many measurements (concentrations, expression, counts) are right-skewed.

The typical pattern:

  • opens correctly: "No, the 95% refers to the method…"
  • explains repeated sampling well
  • then closes with "…so there is a 95% chance the true mean is in your interval"
  • or never says what the interval is useful for (values compatible with the data)
AI as tutor · check its answer
Ask a pair whose AI answer slipped to read out the last paragraph. The lesson is not that AI is bad at statistics; it's that fluent explanations can contradict themselves, and the contradiction usually sits in the summary sentence people remember. For the calculator check: sorting reorders lines but changes no interval, so the count must stay the same. If it changed, the code did something else.

Three things to keep.

  1. A CI is mean ± t × SE: the values reasonably compatible with your data.
  2. The 95% belongs to the method, not to your one interval.
  3. Narrower costs confidence or patients, and small skewed samples under-deliver.
