Day 2 · Topic 1 · Practical statistics for biomedical researchers
Day 2 opening. Yesterday every question got its own test. Today we see that most of those tests are the same model with different inputs. The payoff comes in the next topic: once a test is a model, you can add more variables to it (age, stage, batch), which no t-test can do.

The big picture
The rows appear one by one when the slide opens. The b's are what the model estimates; each comes with a standard error, a CI and a p-value, exactly like yesterday's single numbers. The last row is the reason we bother: the t-test can't adjust for anything, the model can.

The lecture · notebook section B
Three groups need two 0/1 columns ("dummies"), one per non-reference group. The reference group is the one with no column. Every coefficient is therefore a difference from the reference, never a group mean by itself. That's the most common misreading. The ANOVA F-test asks whether b₁ and b₂ are both zero.

Debrief · section A · concept check 2
Ask who guessed "identical". t and p match to every decimal, because they are the same calculation. Welch differs because it lets each group have its own variance. They diverge most when group sizes are very unequal and the smaller group is the more variable one.

Debrief · section B · discussion question 2
The dots are the three group means with 95% CIs; they never move. The arrows are the coefficients: each starts at the reference mean and ends at another group's mean. Switch the reference and the arrows redraw, the table changes, and the F-test doesn't move, because the model's fitted values (the three means) are the same. Discussion: in a paper, pick the reference that makes the comparisons readers care about: the control, the most common group, or the lowest grade.

Debrief · section C
Tumour size against positive nodes. In raw units the slope says "each extra positive node goes with 0.86 mm larger tumours". Switch to standardized units (each variable minus its mean, divided by its SD): the cloud and line don't change shape, but now the slope equals r. Correlation is a regression slope with the units removed. The p-values are identical. Note the r² ≈ 0.11: a "highly significant" relationship that explains about a tenth of the variation.

Three things to keep.

  1. t-test, ANOVA and correlation are one model: outcome ~ predictors.
  2. A group coefficient is a difference from the reference, not a mean.
  3. The model carries the Student t-test's assumptions: equal spread, roughly normal noise.
